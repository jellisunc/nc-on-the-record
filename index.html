U.S. Senate — the open seat

Sen. Thom Tillis is retiring, so nobody in this race is an incumbent. One candidate has an eight-year record as governor to audit; the other has never held elected office. That difference is the race. Outside groups had reported $77.5 million in independent spending on this race through Oct 26 — most of the ads you'll see aren't from the candidates at all. Democrat

Roy Cooper

Governor of NC 2017–2025 · Attorney General 2001–2017 · State senator & representative before that. Age 69.

Says — 2026 campaign

  • Signature promise: "Make Stuff Cost Less" — groceries, health care, utilities, child care, housing; hold Duke Energy accountable. (roycooper.com)
  • Would vote to roll back the Trump tariffs; restore ACA premium tax credits, reverse the 2025 law's Medicaid cuts, expand Medicare drug-price negotiation. (Ballotpedia News)
  • Border security with ICE focused on people who committed violent crimes; says he backed the 2024 bipartisan border bill.
  • Guns: disarm domestic abusers, act on untraceable "ghost guns," ban assault weapons. Defends reproductive rights; points to his veto of the 12-week abortion law.

Did — the record

  • 104 vetoes — 52 overridden. Both are state records. Republicans held veto-proof supermajorities for 4 of his 8 years. (The Assembly)
  • Medicaid expansion: signed HB 76 on March 27, 2023 — while publicly opposing its budget-contingency clause — then let the 2023 budget become law unsigned to trigger it. Coverage started Dec 1, 2023 for roughly 600,000 adults. (governor.nc.gov)
  • Vetoed the 12-week abortion law (SB 20) — overridden, law took effect. Vetoed voter ID (SB 824, after voters approved the amendment 55.5%) — overridden. Vetoed the pistol-permit repeal (SB 41) — overridden. (NC Legislative Library)
  • Vetoed sheriff–ICE cooperation bills in 2019 and 2022 (vetoes sustained) and again in 2024 (overridden).
  • Signed the 2021 bipartisan budget (SB 105): personal income tax cut 5.25% → 3.99% and the corporate income tax phased to 0% by 2030. Also signed the HB2 repeal compromise, the HB 951 clean-energy law, and sports-betting legalization. (NC State Poole College)

Says vs. Did — the check

  • Matches: his health-care promise matches his central documented act as governor — expansion to ~600,000 people, passed with a Republican legislature. His "worked with Republicans" claim is backed by specific signed laws.
  • Tension: he campaigns as a bipartisan dealmaker while holding the state record for vetoes and overrides — many of his stated positions were overridden, not enacted. He campaigns against tax cuts for the wealthy while his signature sits on the budget phasing the corporate tax to zero. His campaign stresses border security; his record includes three ICE-bill vetoes.

Risk check

  • His record is long and fully checkable — but it's an executive record (signing, vetoing, administering). He's never cast votes in Congress; his last legislative service ended in 2001.
  • Because 52 vetoes were overridden, several documented "positions" describe what he tried to do, not what became law.
  • His leverage in Raleigh tracked seat counts: 2019–2022 every veto held; 2023–2024 nearly all were overridden. The same variable would apply in the Senate.
Republican

Michael Whatley

Chairman of the Republican National Committee 2024–2025 · NC Republican Party chair 2019–2024 · Before that: Bush administration energy official, Senate chief of staff, energy-industry lobbyist.

Says — 2026 campaign

  • Entered the race at President Trump's request, with his "Complete and Total Endorsement." Core pledge: "President Trump deserves an ally and North Carolina deserves a strong conservative voice in the Senate. I will be that voice." (AP)
  • Make the Trump tax cuts permanent; no tax on tips, overtime, or Social Security; cut spending; expand energy production. Supports Trump's tariffs (the opposite of Cooper). (michaelwhatley.com)
  • Crime leads his issues page: "back the blue without apology." On immigration: "100% supportive of President Trump securing the border and deporting the millions of criminal illegal aliens."
  • Strongly pro-life; praised the Dobbs decision and NC's 12-week law; endorsed by SBA Pro-Life America. His issues page lists no detailed health-care plan.
No voting record exists. Whatley has never held elected office — this is his first run. There are no votes, budgets, or administered programs to audit. Everything in "Says" above is a promise, an endorsement, or a party-platform position.

Did — the checkable record

  • As RNC chair he issued the party's statement praising the 2025 "One Big Beautiful Bill" as "a huge win for all Americans." The CBO estimates that law cuts about $1 trillion from Medicaid over a decade; NC Newsline put the NC share at roughly $50 billion. (NC Newsline via Digital Free Press)
  • A CNN KFile review found that after the 2020 election he repeatedly claimed "massive fraud" in Milwaukee, Detroit, and Philadelphia. No evidence of massive fraud was found in those cities, and 60+ post-election cases were lost. He later acknowledged Biden as the legitimate president.
  • Tasked by Trump in Jan 2025 with overseeing Hurricane Helene recovery in western NC and named to the FEMA Review Council (whose report recommended shifting flood insurance toward private markets). A Citizen Times public-records review found few documented contacts with state/county recovery officials; Whatley counters with federal funding totals — figures he has cited vary ($5B+ in Sept 2025; $11B claimed on X). (Asheville Citizen Times)
  • Spent roughly a decade as an energy-industry lobbyist (HBW Resources; Consumer Energy Alliance) before party leadership — the private-sector record behind his energy-production platform.

Says vs. Did — the check

  • Matches: his central promise — loyalty to the Trump agenda — is consistent with his documented behavior: six years running Trump's party apparatus at state and national level, and he entered this race at the president's request.
  • Cannot be checked: no specific policy promise (taxes, border, police funding) can be compared against any vote or administered program, because there is none. One opponent claim to treat carefully: that he would back a national abortion ban is inferred from endorsements and platform — it is not stated on his campaign site.

Risk check

  • Promise-based candidacy: nothing to audit. On any issue his campaign hasn't addressed, there is no record to predict from.
  • Alignment is the platform: he has not campaigned on any point of divergence from the president. Expect votes that track the administration's positions — including state-specific effects his opponent is campaigning against.
  • His leadership experience is running party organizations and election litigation — never a government agency or a public budget.

Contested claim  The prison-settlement ad claim

The claim: Whatley's campaign says a Feb 2021 COVID-era prison settlement by Cooper's administration released DeCarlos Brown Jr., the man later charged with the August 2025 Charlotte light-rail killing of Iryna Zarutska. The settlement itself is real — it moved roughly 3,500 prisoners to early release or supervision over COVID conditions.

The official record: the NC Department of Adult Correction states Brown was not released early under the settlement: he completed his mandatory minimum sentence for armed robbery and was released September 20, 2020 — five months before the settlement existed. Weigh the claim against that. Sources: The Assembly; Charlotte Observer fact check

NC Supreme Court — Associate Justice

An eight-year term, and the only seat on the court up this year. The court has a 5–2 Republican majority that this race doesn't change — what it changes is the margin on the cases that split it. One honest complication: these two records are different kinds of record. A judge's record is her opinions. A legislator's record is his or her votes. We show each as it is. (Public Radio East)

Democrat · Incumbent

Anita Earls

On the court since Jan 1, 2019 · Yale Law, 1987 · Deputy Assistant AG in the Clinton Justice Department's Civil Rights Division · Founded the Southern Coalition for Social Justice (2007) after ~30 years as a civil-rights attorney.

Says — judicial philosophy

  • "The Constitution is supreme… even if it means finding that a law passed by the General Assembly was unconstitutional." She names the legislature — not campaign money — as "the biggest threat to judicial independence." (Citizen Times)
  • Second-term focus: North Carolinians' lack of access to legal services; campaigns on "Keeping NC's Courts Fair and Independent."

Did — the rulings

  • Holmes v. Moore (2022) — she wrote it. Her 4–3 majority struck down the SB 824 voter ID law, finding it was enacted with racially discriminatory intent. After the court's membership changed, the new majority reversed her opinion on rehearing in April 2023 and upheld the law; Earls dissented.
  • Harper v. Hall (2022) — joined the majorities striking down the legislature's maps as unconstitutional partisan gerrymanders. Also overruled on rehearing in April 2023; Earls dissented, calling rehearing based on a change in the court's composition a "radical break with 205 years of history."
  • Leandro education case (Apr 2026) — dissented when the majority dismissed the court's ruling that the state must provide a sound basic education, writing that the court damaged "its own legitimacy by trying to rewrite a fundamental constitutional guarantee."
  • State v. Harvey (2019) — her first dissent, in a criminal case: she argued a defendant's self-defense claim should have gone to the jury. Included here because her record isn't only election law.

Risk check

  • Her two highest-profile rulings were both reversed on rehearing after the court's membership flipped — her record is partly a record of the losing side of the current court.
  • She came to the bench after a career advocating one side of the cases she now judges (civil-rights litigation), and co-chaired Gov. Cooper's Task Force for Racial Equity in Criminal Justice — a point her opponent raises. Voters can weigh how much that background predicts her judging.

Whether that is experience or entanglement is the voter's call; the votes are documented above.

The 3 constitutional amendments

No candidates here — you are the decision. Each card quotes the official ballot language (from the session law that placed it on the ballot), then what a YES actually changes in plain English, using the nonpartisan Legislative Services Office summaries. All three were put on the ballot by the General Assembly and pass with a simple majority of votes cast on the question.

Amendment 1 — Photo ID for all voters

"Constitutional amendment to require all voters, not just those presenting to vote in person, to present photo identification before voting."

What changes: the constitution today requires photo ID only from voters "offering to vote in person." This amendment deletes that qualifier, extending the constitutional requirement to every method — including voting by mail.

The context the ads skip: state statute already requires mail voters to include a photocopy of photo ID or an ID Exception Form (in place since 2023, upheld by the NC Supreme Court). So a YES mostly moves an existing rule from statute — which a legislature can change — into the constitution, where it can't be changed without another vote of the people. A NO leaves the constitution as it is; the mail-ballot statute stays unless the legislature repeals it.

Source: Session Law 2024-58 (SB 921)

Amendment 2 — State income tax cap: 7% → 3.5%

"Constitutional amendment to keep the State income tax rate from being raised higher than three and one-half percent (3.5%)."

What changes: the constitutional ceiling on the state income tax drops from 7% to 3.5%, for tax years starting Jan 1, 2027. Per the nonpartisan Legislative Analysis Division, the cap applies to personal and corporate income taxes.

The context the ads skip: this changes nobody's current tax bill. The actual personal rate is 3.99% for 2026 and is already scheduled by statute to fall to 2.99% by 2033; the corporate rate is already headed to 0% by 2030. A YES is a vote about the future: no General Assembly could raise the income tax above 3.5% — in a recession, a disaster, or any emergency — without first getting voters to amend the constitution again. A NO keeps the ceiling at 7% and leaves the scheduled rate cuts untouched either way.

Sources: Session Law 2026-4 (SB 1080); Legislative Analysis Division summary

Amendment 3 — Property tax limits (the one with no number in it)

"Constitutional amendment requiring limits on property tax increases by local governments."

What changes: read the amendment's actual text and you will not find a cap — no percentage, no dollar figure, no formula. It adds one sentence to the constitution ordering the General Assembly to "enact general laws limiting the amount by which the levy of taxes on property may increase, which may include exceptions." The limit itself would be written later, by a future legislature, with whatever exceptions it chooses.

Two distinctions that matter: (1) it limits growth in a local government's total levy (total collections), which is not the same as capping your individual tax bill; (2) a statutory cap already exists — state law caps most county and city property tax rates at $1.50 per $100 of value (G.S. 153A-149 / 160A-209), though rates and levy growth are different levers. A YES votes for a limit that does not yet exist in any specific form. A NO leaves levy growth governed by current law and local elected boards.

Sources: Session Law 2026-5 (HB 1089); nonpartisan legislative staff analysis

How this site works

Unaffiliated voters are North Carolina's largest registration group — and the voters that campaign ads, which are mostly funded by outside groups, are least honest with. This site exists to put the record in one place.

  • "Says" is quoted or closely paraphrased from the candidate's own campaign site and statements. Their words, not our summary of them.
  • "Did" comes from official records: veto and bill histories at ncleg.gov, court opinions, FEC filings, and statements the candidate made in prior public roles — each linked so you can check it yourself.
  • Same structure, same questions, every candidate — regardless of party. If a candidate has no record to check, we say so plainly. A missing record is information; it is not a verdict.
  • Contested claims are shown as claims, next to the official record, and labeled.
  • No endorsements, no advocacy-group scorecards, no horserace polls, no adjectives doing the work that facts should do.
  • Spotted an error? Every fact here carries its source — if the source says otherwise, the source wins and we correct the page.
